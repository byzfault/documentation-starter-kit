## Method

**Documentation is a product.** It needs information architecture, a maintenance owner, a release process and a feedback loop. Treated as a chore at the end of a sprint, it rots inside a quarter.

**Clarity before cleverness.** The goal is a reader who can act. Where precision and plain language pull apart, I keep the precision and rewrite the sentence.

**Write from the system, not about it.** I read the code, run the product and test the procedure first. That is why the first draft is usually accurate, and why I find the gaps the engineering team had stopped seeing.

**Fix the structure first.** Mixed content types and scattered prerequisites are the reason a guide is hard to use. Editing sentences inside a broken structure is expensive and it does not work.

**Structure for retrieval.** Self-contained topics, explicit headings, no meaning buried in the transition between paragraphs. That has always been right for a reader scanning a page. It is now also what makes content usable by the internal assistants that enterprise clients are deploying over vendor documentation.

## Before and after

A typical reference entry:

> The `transfer` function moves tokens from one address to another. It takes two parameters: `recipient` (address) and `amount` (uint256).

How I would write it:

> **Transferring tokens**
>
> Use `transfer()` to send tokens to another address:
>
> ```solidity
> token.transfer(recipient, amount);
> ```
>
> **Parameters:**
> - `recipient`: destination address, which must be a valid Ethereum address
> - `amount`: number of tokens to transfer, in the smallest unit
>
> **Important:** this reverts if your balance is insufficient. Check `balanceOf()` first when handling user input.
>
> **Common pitfalls:**
> - Forgetting to approve tokens for contracts
> - Not handling transaction failures
> - Skipping address validation before transferring

The context arrives before the code, the example runs, the failure case is named, and the reader knows what to do next.
